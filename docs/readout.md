# Decision memo: room on the cut-off

**To:** Head of Credit Policy
**From:** Credit Risk Analyst, Consumer Lending
**Date:** 3 November 2026
**Subject:** Room on the cut-off: tighten now, test before loosening, trial the new model

## Bottom line

On the current scorecard there is room to tighten and none we can prove to loosen. The approvals it should stop making defaulted at 14.9%, against 4.41% for the book. A cut-off set from what a wrong decision costs, run on the new model, would decline 9.3% of our January to March 2020 approvals. It would lower the book's bad rate to 3.17% and add 92.3M CU a quarter against today's policy. The 95% range from repeated samples is 86.0M to 98.7M. Across the margin assumptions, it is 56.6M to 147.7M. At the scorecard's bad rate instead, the new model approves 3.3 percentage points more applicants, with a range of 2.9 to 3.6. That is about 2,458 booked loans a month. Alternatively, modelled credit losses fall 22% at the same approval rate, with a range of 20% to 23%. These comparisons were matched after the fact on booked test loans. Each needs its own cut-off fixed in advance and its own test. We cannot tell whether we can approve people we reject today, because their outcomes were never observed. A test of 610 approved loans just below today's approval line would answer it. Expected net losses are about 2.7M CU on the declined tail's economics. Losses are likely higher because today's rejects are probably riskier.

## What we recommend

Test quarter, kept separate from model development (January to March 2020, 222,681 booked loans); money in the dataset's currency units (CU). The comparison scorecard is a stand-in we rebuilt because the lender's incumbent model is not in the data. Gains are measured against today's policy, which approved every loan in this book (a 0.5 cut-off approves 99.98% of it and lands in almost the same place); intervals are 95% ranges from 200 repeated samples of the test quarter. Monthly volumes are booked loans: the review queue and test durations must be reset on all applications received, which are not in our data.

## Why

- A bad loan is assumed to cost 60% of its amount; a wrongly declined good customer costs the product's margin (20% cash, 10% revolving, 5% point-of-sale). Today's policy approved every loan in this book; at the assumed 60% loss rate, defaults imply 393.3M CU of modelled losses on the quarter (a 0.5 cut-off approves 99.98% of the book and implies almost all of it, 391.4M CU).
- The rule on the new model declines 20,627 loans, representing 1,062.4M CU lent, that defaulted at 16.6%. It avoids 139.2M CU of modelled losses and gives up 46.9M CU of margin, for a net gain of 92.3M CU. Monthly net gains are 29.4M in January, 37.0M in February and 25.8M in March. On the scorecard the same rule declines 7.5% (bad rate 14.9%), worth 45.9M CU, leaving the book at 3.57%. Most of the new model's edge comes from using more of the available information: 90 inputs against the scorecard's 16. The method alone does not explain it. Letting the existing scorecard use more customer information closes only about a quarter of the gap in ranking accuracy. Switching the rule from the scorecard to the new model moves 8,708 loans from approved to declined, the swap-outs. They total 576.0M CU and defaulted at 14.7%. The 4,776 swap-ins move from declined to approved. They total 150.4M CU and defaulted at 7.1%. The bad-rate gap is 7.6 percentage points, with a range of 6.6 to 8.6.
- Approval rates: 90.8% women, 90.6% men; the lowest age group's rate (ages 18-24) is 0.89 times the highest, above the four-fifths screen of 0.80. Risk estimates for men and ages 18-24 are about 73% of actual default rates; employment status does not fix it and stays out. Passing the approval-rate screen does not resolve this gap; Compliance must confirm lawful remedies because the jurisdiction is undisclosed. Using the 90 inputs, a separate model ranks an applicant aged 50+ above a younger applicant in 84% of pairs. This falls to 78% without years with the current employer. It is 83% without product type and share of past cash applications. It remains 84% without the 7 loan-size, payment and income inputs.

## What the data cannot tell us, and how we will find out

Every loan in the data was approved under the old policy, so the approval rates above are shares of previously approved applicants and the bad rate of past rejects is unknown: tightening is measured, loosening is not. A declined tail loan funds more than one new approval at an unchanged book bad rate only if the newcomers default less often than the tail's 16.6%: 2.8 new approvals per declined loan if they default at 8.8%, 1.5 at 12.5%, 1.4 at 13.2%, one for one (no net gain) at 16.6%. The below-cut-off test uses 610 approved loans from applicants just below today's approval line, those the current policy rejects. It passes if at most 83 default (13.6%); it passes 82% of the time if the true bad rate is the 25%-lower target of 12.5%. If the true rate is at that target, each declined tail loan funds about 1.5 new approvals, about 10,600 more loans a quarter. Expansion would be sized on the measured rate. The expected net loss is about 4,474 CU a loan, or 2.7M CU in all, priced on our declined tail. This is likely an underestimate because today's rejects are probably riskier. The test's duration depends on today's reject volume, which is not in our data.

The historical check of the monitoring plan on April to October 2020 is illustrative and uses hindsight. Two employment fields were dropped partly because their collection stopped in June 2020. Inputs that had already shifted by the test quarter were put on the list of inputs to monitor. In April 2020 volume fell about 86% in one month (60,748 loans in March, 8,289 in April) and was back at March's level by September (61,905; the data end on 5 October), while the bad rate halved in one step and then stayed flat. The lower bad rate may reflect COVID-era measures, the lender's own tightening or loans too recent to have defaulted (the outcome horizon is not disclosed); the data cannot separate these.

## What we need from you

1. Adopt the cost-based rule (you), once Finance confirms the margin, loss and exposure assumptions.
2. Approve the 20% controlled trial of the two-tier cost rules and its written decision rule (you), after Model Validation's review. Agree the proposed bad-rate tolerance of 0.2 percentage points before launch (`config.yaml`, `proposals`).
3. Approve the below-cut-off test and its expected net loss of about 2.7M CU on the declined tail's economics, likely more because today's rejects are probably riskier (you, Finance).
4. Underwriting Operations: confirm review capacity at 5% of each month's applications, averaging 3,711 cases a month on booked volume. No month should exceed its cap. Confirm cost per case, assumed at 300 CU. Pilot separately from the trial groups and measure the share of bad applicants declined before counting the gain.
5. Sign off excluded attributes, decline reasons and lawful remedies for the risk under-prediction by sex and age (Compliance).

## Proposed timeline (to be agreed with the owners)

- Weeks 1 to 4: Finance confirms the cost assumptions. Model Validation reviews the new model. Compliance reviews attributes and decline reasons. Sign decision rules for both tests and build monitoring. Refresh risk estimates on the latest loans old enough to have their final outcome. Estimates are about 10% low for the new model and 14% low for the scorecard on the test quarter.
- Months 2 to 3: the trial collects 20,544 approved loans in the new model group and 82,176 in the scorecard group. This is about 1.5 months on booked volume; the calendar is set by all applications received. The below-cut-off test runs alongside until 610 loans are booked.
- Month 3 on book: early read from 30+ days past due; after the outcome window, go/no-go on the model switch and on any loosening.
